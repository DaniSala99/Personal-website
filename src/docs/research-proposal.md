# PhD Project Plan

**Project:** Forest-Water Relations During Drought: Isotopic and Micrometeorological Quantification of Minor Water Pathways in Stand-Scale Forest Water Budgets  
**Author:** Daniele Sala  
**Date:** June 2026  
**Programme:** ForFuS Doctoral Training Unit, Luxembourg Institute of Science and Technology (LIST)

---

## Abstract

Forest water budgets are poorly constrained at the process level during drought: current evapotranspiration partitioning frameworks assign the dominant flux to canopy transpiration and leave forest floor evaporation as a residual absorbed by measurement error, while foliar water uptake — the direct absorption of water through leaf surfaces during fog, dew, or wetting events — remains essentially absent from operational models. This PhD will quantify these under-constrained water pathways using stable isotopic tracers (δ¹⁸O, δ²H) and sub-canopy micrometeorological monitoring across experimental forest stands in Luxembourg, Switzerland, and the Netherlands, and integrate the resulting process understanding into a stand-scale ecohydrological modelling framework within the ForFuS interdisciplinary doctoral training unit at LIST.

---

## 1. Problem Statement and Motivation

The dominant evapotranspiration partitioning frameworks used in forest hydrology — **Penman-Monteith** and **Shuttleworth-Wallace** — rest on the assumption that canopy transpiration accounts for the majority of the forest water flux. Forest floor evaporation, the evaporation from litter, moss, and bare soil beneath the canopy, is routinely treated as a minor residual. In practice, stand-level eddy covariance datasets frequently exhibit energy balance non-closure on the order of 10–30% of net radiation, and forest floor evaporation is precisely the term most likely to be subsumed into this residual error. The result is that one of the pathways most sensitive to drought-induced canopy thinning and understorey change is also the least well constrained observationally.

Foliar water uptake — direct leaf-surface absorption during fog, dew, or rain-on-drought events — adds a further dimension that existing measurement frameworks cannot resolve independently. During severe water stress, when soil water potential drops below a critical threshold, the hydraulic gradient along the soil-plant-atmosphere continuum can reverse at the leaf-atmosphere interface, making the canopy a transient net water importer. Neither **sap flow sensors** (which measure xylem water flux, not foliar absorption) nor **above-canopy eddy covariance** (which integrates over the entire canopy-atmosphere interface) can isolate this pathway in isolation. Stable water isotopes offer a tracer-based route around this measurement barrier, but their application to distinguishing foliar uptake from transpiration remains methodologically contested.

The frequency and severity of drought events in central Europe has increased markedly in recent decades, with documented consequences for forest water status and mortality risk that remain difficult to predict at stand scale [*CITATION TO VERIFY — drought-induced tree mortality literature*]. Understanding whether and how forests exploit these minor water pathways during stress has direct implications for models of forest vulnerability and resilience, and for silvicultural management under projected future drought regimes.

---

## 2. Research Questions

**RQ1.** To what extent do **foliar water uptake** and **forest floor evaporation** contribute to the total water balance of a forest stand during drought events, and how does the magnitude and timing of these contributions vary across the three experimental sites (Luxembourg, Switzerland, Netherlands)?

**RQ2.** Can **stable isotopic tracers** (δ¹⁸O, δ²H) reliably separate foliar water uptake and forest floor evaporation from the dominant transpiration signal, and what are the methodological limitations of the **two water worlds framework** when applied to these minor water pathways under drought conditions?

**RQ3.** How can quantitative estimates of these minor water fluxes be integrated into a **stand-scale ecohydrological model**, and what do the resulting process constraints imply for the simulated resilience of forest water budgets to drought?

## 3. Approach and Work Packages

### WP1 — Field Instrumentation and Multi-site Data Collection
Deployment and maintenance of micrometeorological monitoring stations, isotopic sampling networks (precipitation, throughfall, soil water at multiple depths, xylem water), and sap flow sensors across the three experimental sites in Luxembourg, Switzerland, and the Netherlands. By the end of Year 1 this work package delivers a uniquely comprehensive, harmonised multi-site dataset as a standalone scientific output — publishable as a data or methods paper regardless of downstream modelling outcomes, and providing the observational backbone that all subsequent work packages depend on.

### WP2 — Isotopic Analysis and Water Source Separation
Laboratory analysis of δ¹⁸O and δ²H ratios from field samples, and application of isotopic mixing models implemented in a Bayesian framework to propagate sampling and analytical uncertainty through to source proportion estimates. This work package also evaluates the performance and limitations of the two water worlds framework for distinguishing foliar uptake and forest floor contributions from the dominant transpiration signal.

### WP3 — Energetic Quantification of Forest Floor Evaporation
Measurement of forest floor evaporation using sub-canopy eddy covariance and Bowen ratio energy balance methods. Cross-validation between the energy balance approach and the isotopic mass balance from WP2 provides methodological redundancy: agreement between the two independent methods validates both; divergence is itself informative about systematic biases in sub-canopy turbulence measurement or isotopic mixing assumptions.

### WP4 — Integration into a Stand-Scale Ecohydrological Model
Development of process representations of foliar water uptake and forest floor evaporation within a stand-scale ecohydrological modelling framework. The model will be calibrated and evaluated against the multi-site observational dataset from WP1–3, with particular attention to its ability to reproduce observed isotopic signatures and micrometeorological flux partitioning under varying drought intensities.

### WP5 — Inter-site Synthesis and Forest Resilience Implications
Comparative analysis across the three sites to identify the climate, species composition, and soil factors that govern the relative importance of foliar uptake and forest floor evaporation during drought. Synthesis of implications for forest drought resilience modelling and silvicultural management, including a contribution to the ForFuS interdisciplinary training programme's cross-disciplinary outputs.

## 4. Expected Challenges and Mitigations

| Challenge | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| **Energy balance closure** — sub-canopy EC residuals of 10–30% of net radiation can mask the forest floor evaporation signal | Cross-validation with isotopic mass balance estimates from WP2; convergence validates the approach, divergence is itself scientifically informative |
| **Xylem water extraction** — cryogenic extraction may introduce systematic bias from cellulose-bound water contamination | Triangulation between cryogenic extraction, direct vapour equilibration, and published isotopic correction procedures; multi-method comparison on a subset of samples |
| **Inter-site heterogeneity** — differences in species composition, soil properties, and regional climate between the three sites limit direct comparability | Standardised field measurement and sample processing protocols across all sites; hierarchical statistical model treating site identity as a random effect |
| **Stand vs. tree scale** — within-stand variability in foliar uptake and sap flow between size classes complicates stand-level upscaling | Stratified sampling by diameter class and dominant species; geostatistical upscaling with explicit uncertainty bounds propagated from tree-level measurements |

## 6. Background and Added Value

My M.Sc. thesis at Politecnico di Milano — building, calibrating, and running sensitivity and uncertainty analysis on the FEST process-based hydrological model over the Po basin — gave me direct experience of the methodological tension this PhD addresses from the modelling side: constraining poorly identifiable sub-processes from aggregate or indirect observations. In the Po catchment, the individual contributions of surface runoff, interflow, and deep percolation cannot be measured directly at the spatial and temporal resolution needed for model calibration; they must be inferred from the integrated streamflow signal and from spatial patterns in soil moisture and groundwater levels. The challenge of separating foliar water uptake and forest floor evaporation from a bulk eddy covariance flux is structurally analogous — and the solution strategy (multi-source observations, Bayesian uncertainty propagation, cross-validation between independent methods) is the same.

My current role at Progesi S.p.A. as a Natural Risk Assessment Specialist involves the operational integration of data from heterogeneous sources — numerical weather prediction output from ICON-I2 and ECMWF-IFS, ground-based sensor networks, and satellite-derived products — under real-time constraints and with direct operational consequences. Coordinating a field monitoring network across three international sites, managing sample collection logistics, and quality-controlling multi-source timeseries data are operational demands for which this role has directly prepared me.

Isotope hydrology and ecophysiological instrumentation — xylem water extraction techniques, sub-canopy eddy covariance system configuration, and sap flow measurement methods — are competences I will develop during the doctoral programme under Prof. Keim's supervision at LIST. I do not present them as existing skills. The quantitative contribution I bring to the project from day one is in process-based hydrological modelling, multi-source data integration, and uncertainty-aware calibration: a methodological foundation from which the field-specific techniques of isotope ecohydrology can be systematically acquired.
